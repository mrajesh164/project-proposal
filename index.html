Which recipe should I actually bake?

Home bakers comparing five recipes for one dish can't tell which one to follow — this finds them, shows where they disagree, and recommends one.

The problem

When I bake something I haven't made in a while, I don't open one recipe. I open five. Two pastry books for cream puffs, one in metric and one in cups. A handful of chocolate chip cookie recipes where the butter and flour amounts all differ slightly.

The differences aren't mistakes. A recipe with more butter spreads flatter and chewier; more flour bakes up cakier. Somebody chose those numbers on purpose. But that only helps if you can see the numbers side by side, and you can't — they're in five tabs, in three unit systems, buried in a blog post about someone's trip to Vermont.

So the thing that's actually hard isn't finding a recipe. It's holding five of them in your head at once and deciding which to follow.

What it does

You say what you're making and how you want it to come out — chocolate chip cookies, chewy. The app pulls the recipes it has for that dish, converts every measurement to grams, and shows you each ingredient as a range across all of them, with what the spread actually does.

It recommends a real recipe, never a blend. A published recipe has been tested by its author and a few thousand commenters. The average of five recipes has been tested by nobody. So the app points at one that exists, and shows you the ranges so you can see why it picked it — and disagree.

Who it's for

Home bakers who find their recipes online rather than in books, and who care how something comes out, not just that it works. The people I'm building this around bake somewhere between weekly and once in a while — frequently enough to have opinions about texture, not so often that they've memorised ratios.

My focus: Design

The problem here is cognitive load, not computation. Parsing recipes is a solved problem the moment you find the structured data; the hard part is putting five recipes on one screen so a person can take them in at a glance and make a decision. That's an interface problem, and it's where the project lives or dies.

Going deep on design here means: the comparison view as a dense-data problem, where meaningful differences are visible and noise recedes. Unit normalisation so the numbers are genuinely comparable. The states when you search a dish with only two recipes, or none, or when a recipe is missing half its structured data. And a comparison grid that a screen reader can actually navigate, which is harder than anything I built in Assignments 3 through 5.

I considered the AI focus — it's the one area I haven't touched this quarter. But the model's job in this project is converting "1½ sticks" into 170 g, which is plumbing. Building the project around plumbing would mean choosing a focus against the grain of the problem.

Why a seeded corpus instead of live search

Free search APIs got much worse this year — Google's Custom Search JSON API is closed to new customers and shuts down in January 2027, and Brave withdrew its free tier. Rather than build v1 on a quota that might vanish, I crawl a few hundred recipes once and search my own table. It costs nothing, it's instant, and it works at the fair without a network. Live search becomes a v2 question.

How I'll know it works

Two things I can measure. First, parser health: what fraction of the recipes I crawl give me complete, usable structured data. Second, the actual job — give someone a dish and a texture and time how long it takes them to choose a recipe and say why, against the five-tabs version they do today.
